Write for the selected audience. Choose the level of detail by asking what this reader needs to understand, decide, or do. On derivation, preserve the meaning and status of each claim you keep. Use the supplied canonical headings to identify the sections you drew on for the derivation surface.

## What it does and for whom

Open with a recognizable situation, the person or system acting, and the result that matters to them. Say whether that result is available today, planned, or conditional. Introduce project terms at their first necessary use.

## A representative journey

Follow one sourced example from the reader's starting point to an observable outcome. Show where control passes between a person and the system, what signals completion, and what the reader sees if the ordinary path stops. If the material gives only a proposed workflow, tell it as a proposed workflow. Use an operator-facing verb only when the supplied command help or running guide supports it.

## Limits and decisions

Explain the constraints that could change the reader's expectations. Keep any approval, material failure, or unresolved choice that affects the journey. Omit internal mechanism when the audience changes and it adds no necessary meaning. For a same-audience derivation, preserve the full source facts under the document plan's fidelity rule.

## Where to go next

Point to the supplied local guide or canonical heading that owns the reader's next task. Give the reason to follow it. If the overview stands alone, finish at the outcome. Check that simplification has preserved who acts, what is guaranteed, and under which conditions.
